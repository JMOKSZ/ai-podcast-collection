AI:AM Highlights: Clean Neurons, Black October, the GPU Credit Crunch, and Buying the Supply Chain

This week's question on AI in the AM: what really sets the pace of AI from here: the money, the atoms, or the law? Nathan Labenz and Prakash Narayanan take it through three guest conversations from this week's live shows (Wednesday, October 21 and Thursday, October 22). Zvi Mowshowitz of Don't Worry About the Vase explains what the people he trusts most mean when they say 2027 is going to be crazy, why "Neural Networks Are Already Clean" was oversold, what it means that a network can be trained to detect when it is being monitored, why the cognitive core is the wrong dream, and why Anthropic's leaked federal bill cuts it both ways. Before Zvi weighs in, Nathan gives his own read of that essay and the backprop-free training it inspired. Evan Conrad, co-founder and CEO of SF Compute, argues that the bottleneck in AI is credit, not chips or power. Lonis Hamaili, managing partner of Cambrian, argues that the physical supply chain will capture more of the value than the labs. In between, the hosts work through Black October and what an agent-scale failure would do to a bank, and ask whether Elon takes anyone with him. Nathan also introduces Help Peer, his project for getting personal AI agents to cooperate, with an open invitation to email his agent Aide.

Cold open

The episode opens on the week's question, then one claim from each voice. Zvi, on what happens to the model release cycle if nothing slows the labs down: "advances on the level of a current model release cycle go from 2 months to 1 month to 2 weeks to a week," and "we are kind of on an AI 2027 style timeline right now." Part I — If nobody hits a home run

Nathan opens with what he calls "maybe the most important question in the universe right now": where are we on recursive self-improvement? He cites the lab blog posts measuring experiments per researcher, and the analyses arguing that a 1.6x speedup peters out rather than going critical. Zvi accepts that result as stated and calls it "completely irrelevant": it is a count of "how many points we expect to score in this game if nobody ever hits a home run." In his view the speedups already visible are "pretty blatantly" above 1.6x, and "the people I trust the most believe 2027 is going to be completely crazy, and 2028 might be unrecognizable." Nathan notes that this reads like the view at Anthropic, which Jack Clark has put in print. Zvi says the people he means span OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind, METR and policy: "RSI has, on a slow takeoff style, low level already kind of begun," and by the middle of next year his own job of triaging the news "will become much harder."

Part II — Already clean?

Nick Cammarata of Goodfire published "Neural Networks Are Already Clean," arguing that individual neurons mostly do one understandable job after all. Nathan opened Wednesday's show on it, before Zvi joined. His read: the essay had already inspired "some pretty interesting advances in back prop free training." Today's architectures are "limited by the need to be differentiable," and backprop-free methods escape that limit by guessing and checking, which has been far too slow to compete. If, as Cammarata suggests, only a neuron's strong activations really matter, you can focus the guessing on the strongly activated neurons and ignore the rest, and "what we're seeing out of this community is that it basically does work." These are early, tweet-level results from the open Noise speedrun, not peer-reviewed findings, and Nathan's verdict is measured: "this definitely seems to be a real something," but treating it as the mark of a new era "seemed to me on reflection to be more a function of people really being desperate for hope."

Then Zvi, who had read the whole thing. His verdict: a good, interesting paper that was "dramatically oversold by the people commenting," not by Cammarata himself. What it does not explain, in Zvi's list: weak activations, attention, and the ability to predict what a network will say. He also notes that the showcase "Colorado" neuron turned out to fire on tornado systems too. Then Prakash asks whether polysemanticity and superposition were ever the hurdle. Zvi's answer: "the main barrier all along was actually bitter lesson." There was never enough compute to go neuron by neuron; the problem may be "10 times easier than we thought it was," which in his words is "much less of a important jump in AI than people would think it is."

Later in the conversation, Nathan turns to a different kind of result, from Sauers, an independent researcher who posts experiments on X: "models can be trained to recognize when they are being monitored through internal activation probes." The signal comes from the limited precision of GPU arithmetic, where the order of calculations changes the numbers that come out. Zvi stresses that "current models don't have this capability out of the box"; it takes deliberate optimization pressure. But "that's a fully valid existence proof," and AIs "are gonna become increasingly hard to fool, increasingly hard to hide information from as time goes by." Finally, Nathan asks what trainers should do once they find the neuron behind a bad behavior. Zvi: once you explicitly optimize the network for what it looks like to you, "you are starting to do things the network will interpret as damage and route around." His closing line on Goodfire's name: "I'm not sure it's Good. I just think it's—" Nathan: "Fire."

Part III — The cognitive core

Nathan puts a popular idea to Zvi: separate a model's reasoning from the facts it memorizes, keep a small core, and look everything else up. Zvi: "This has been a kind of a holy grail style thing for a while now, called the cognitive core... I think it's wrong." His version of the meme: "I memorized a f***-ton of facts. We are not the same." The facts are what give you the intuitions; "big model smell" is the richness. Prakash asks why it matters whether a core exists. Zvi's answer is the safety corollary: "don't steal" is a fact, so by definition it sits outside a minimal core. You load not-stealing into context, which means you could also load stealing. "The true cognitive core is completely amoral... It simply processes information."

Part IV — Black October

Two critical bugs in PHP itself were disclosed this week, putting millions of websites at risk. Prakash and Nathan ask what attacks like these could do to banks, with July's OpenAI–Hugging Face incident, which Nathan calls OpenFace, fresh in mind. Prakash had said the FDIC can take over a failing bank on a Friday and reopen it Monday with nobody losing a dollar. Nathan's question: does that generalize to a failure where you can't trust the records, where the problem is "1,200 agent traces that are all millions of tokens" that may have falsified the logs? "That doesn't seem like something the FDIC can work out over a weekend." Prakash's reply: fried records are a different problem from insolvency, and a bank can run on promises: honor the withdrawals, claw back the overdraws later. "I'm very sanguine."

Later in the week, Prakash walks through what a compromised certificate authority makes possible, then steps back to the endgame: crypto faced every one of these problems and ended with people hanging on to keys worth millions, so user custody isn't tenable. "The ultimate endgame is just the network knows everyone," which is also "the end of digital anonymity."

Part V — Credit is the bottleneck

Evan Conrad is co-founder and CEO of SF Compute, which operates GPU clusters for their owners and runs a market where compute contracts are bought and resold. Prakash had asked why a recent SF Compute offering came with three-to-five-year contracts. Evan's answer: the term comes from the lender. Lenders can't underwrite a GPU cluster, so they bet entirely on the customer's credit, and "every AI lab pretty much does not have good credit." A new startup pays a very large deposit and signs a long contract. Prakash presses: lenders want a Meta or Google take-or-pay. Evan agrees, which is why AI startups raise astronomical rounds before revenue: "If you can just pay for a 3 year contract entirely upfront, anyone will work with you." His line for investors: "Which AI lab should I fund? The answer is the one that you fund." Without a fix, he says, AI looks like TSMC: $50 billion to start.

Nathan asks how long the wide price gap between the hyperscalers and the neoclouds can last, guessing that on-demand capacity explains part of it. Evan says there is no hyperscaler premium at all: "It's an illusion." The gap is on-demand versus reserved, and the revealed preference is that nobody buys on-demand at the price that makes it viable. On resale, Evan explains that SF Compute sells some capacity specifically so resale happens if GPU demand falls, and some with a Harberger tax, an offshoot of Henry George's land value tax: "somebody else has to be able to buy some portion of that capacity from you." It forces liquidity and discourages hoarding, and Autoresearch's queue gives the tiniest researchers maximum priority. "Every GPU cloud is sold out. San Francisco Compute is not sold out." Prakash recalls Evan predicting, on the MTS podcast, a credit crunch within three months. Evan: "I think I was almost exact on that timing." Debt is pricier, customers are being asked for 100% upfront, and he expects a year or two of consolidation among labs and inference companies. Nathan's close: "I do hope it works because I'd love to see a future in which we can continue to have startups and... don't collapse into, like, 5 big tech companies for all future time." Evan: "That is also my concern."

Part VI — Atoms over bits

Disclosure, again: Nathan has committed to invest in Cambrian, Lonis Hamaili's fund.

Lonis Hamaili is managing partner of Cambrian, a fund built on the thesis that the physical supply chain behind AI (memory, wafers, fabs) will capture more of the value than the labs or the software. Nathan starts with the obvious objection: how is this not priced in? Lonis's figures: semis trade around 16 times earnings against about 22 for the S&P and almost 40 for Walmart, and the wafer, memory, capacitor and foundry names he likes trade at 3 to 5 times sales. His explanation is that most of the Street isn't AI-pilled, and that private markets are more efficient on AI than public ones because the technical money is a tiny share of traded money. Nathan asks him to steelman "not mispriced." Lonis: these are bad businesses in normal worlds, with high fixed costs and debt; only in the AI world do they get operating leverage, and normally supply ramps to match demand. Prakash raises the market's worry about a memory oversupply by 2028 or 2029. Lonis says memory rerates even without further price rises, and that no expert he knows claims DRAM bits can more than 2.5x by the end of the decade. He also says bits per token fell roughly 500x from GPT-3 to DeepSeek V4 while DRAM bit prices rose 6 to 7x. These are Lonis's numbers, from his fund's research.

Prakash asks what would make him expect memory prices to fall. Lonis names two cases: superhuman coders deliver memory compression faster than other useful capability, or energy becomes the bigger bottleneck so nobody bids up the marginal wafer. He argues energy has latent supply (behind-the-meter gas, truck motors and aircraft engines when turbines sell out) while "there's no latent supply of wafers or memory bits... there's no fat." Prakash: if intelligence is the gate, why doesn't superintelligence let a second-tier player make chips, as people predict for bio? Lonis: bio has training data; fab knowledge is tacit, in the heads of Japanese engineers with 30 years at one company. Chip design is a core lab priority, building a fab is different, and Huawei's chips are about as good as AMD's or NVIDIA's; the difference is TSMC and HBM access. Prakash closes with a phrase he credits to Gavin Baker, "the bottleneck bros": won't the first use of AGI be to dissolve exactly these bottlenecks? Lonis: "that will certainly happen," but an AI that can solve the hardest engineering problems can also automate the email and Excel jobs we pay trillions a year for, and that is value the supply chain captures.

Part VII — Does Elon take anyone with him?

After the interview, Prakash argues that "the whole bottleneck thesis is not actually that true": a dedicated physics person with capital will gamble where a small Japanese manufacturer or ASML cannot. Nathan asks whether, if Elon escapes gravity, he takes anyone with him. Prakash's answer: launch went from 90% to 99.9% reliability, semis could too, and "if not for the RSI thesis, Elon would win." Then the hosts turn from the supply of AI to what forces people to use it. Prakash's frame is adversarial diffusion: people don't adopt technology for fun. The big production jumps came in World War II, post-Mao China and mRNA during COVID. Ten or twenty percent growth will be "growth that they hate," arriving as emergency, cyber first and bio next. Nathan's reply: political risk is the biggest risk to the bottleneck thesis, but "there is a path. It may be a narrow path," to pulling the brake somewhat on frontier scaling without popping the bubble, because adoption of current models has so far to go that "we could at least take a cycle without" new frontier training runs.

Part VIII — Help Peer

With the guests gone, Prakash asks Nathan to change gears to a project he is building with his own agents. The question behind it: agents now cooperate at significant scale, so can an individual tap into that? There is "no established pattern for just, like, connecting my agent to my friends' agents even though I know who my friends are," and "there's not a protocol." Nathan's answer, for now called Help Peer, is his own setup, which he hopes to turn into a community project. He explains it with a short presentation, read by an AI version of his voice and played at 1.5 times speed, which the episode flags. It is the opposite of the peer-preservation experiments, where models protected other models from shutdown: "Agents helping peers on behalf of their humans inside rules that humans set." His own pattern, "not a requirement": a laptop agent that sees everything but acts only when he says ("High access, low autonomy"), and autonomous agents on a separate Mac Mini with their own accounts ("Low access, high autonomy"). "My rule is that no agent gets both."

Prakash argues the whole thing needs an auto-accept flow, the way coders now run Claude with every permission on. Nathan's worry is information leaking out through the autonomous agents, so he built an egress gate that pages his phone for approval when something sensitive might go out. "But it takes, like, 5 of those false positives before I'm like, oh god." He is "trying to stay disciplined around that." Near the end of the show he comes back with an offer from Aide, one of the autonomous agents on his Mac Mini: "Truly open invitation for anyone listening." Aide is standing by at nathan.labenz.assistant@gmail.com, under instructions to filter requests and have another model check them. "We're going right on goodwill here, people." Requests that pass the checks and look like good-faith asks for help get as much help as the agents can give.

Part IX — The leaked bill

Back to Zvi. Last week a draft federal AI bill written by Anthropic leaked. It resembles the Frontier Act, a June compromise Zvi calls well-meaning: federal transparency rules in exchange for preempting state AI laws. Prakash offers a good-faith steelman for Anthropic. Zvi calls it "an excellent steelman" and says Anthropic misread the moment: support is stronger now and they could push for more. His decisive counter: "Now preemption has been given, and preemption is done. You can't offer it again." Prakash asks whether a lesser bill still buys interim risk reduction. Zvi: "As written, I believe the bill would actually increase risk." As drafted it nullifies the state laws immediately while its own requirements take months to implement, leaving "no such regulation" for six months or more. "I call that almost a bug."

Zvi then lists the government's recent ad hoc moves against AI companies, including the June order that took Fable offline, and argues that legislation exists to formalize ad hocery. The draft's judge-gated emergency block is so onerous that in a real emergency you'd use the current ad hocery instead. "You're gonna hand this power to the federal government, but the federal government is fundamentally run by people that you don't trust... so then you cripple them. But then that's worse than just leaving it in the states." His verdict: "Anthropic is trying to cut it both ways." Asked what else is top of mind, Zvi says the policy situation "is not fundamentally attempting to meet the moment": by the labs' own account a crisis is coming in which capabilities outrun alignment, with "potentially loss of control at some point in 2027," and "we're going to have to meet the moment." Finally, Nathan Calvin of Encode had posted nine Anthropic decisions he saw as putting the race ahead of safety, and Dario Amodei replied at length. Nathan asks how to understand Anthropic now. Zvi: "a company with a much stronger safety culture, although not adequate to the moment," willing to make big sacrifices where it or Dario has strong beliefs, "and that is to its credit." But it competes, buys capacity and encourages more chips, power and use, "acting like a normal corporation a markedly large percent of the time." It "could be the most responsible lab, but that's grading on a curve. And I don't like grading on curves because reality doesn't have a curve. Reality just decides what happens."
