This is a rare two-part episode. Nathan first sat down with Alan Chan, a Research Fellow at GovAI (the Centre for the Governance of AI), on September 22. They went the full 90 minutes and, in Nathan's words, "it still felt short somehow." Six days later, on September 28, Alan published What if automating AI R&D triggers an intelligence explosion?, a paper he led whose 22 authors include OpenAI chief scientist Jakub Pachocki and Anthropic co-founder Jack Clark, alongside Geoffrey Hinton, Yoshua Bengio, Andrew Barto, Eric Horvitz, Dawn Song, Hilary Greaves and Anton Korinek. So Nathan brought him back on September 30 for a part 2, which follows part 1 directly.

Part 1: governing AI agents

Alan has spent the last two years writing the papers the rest of us are now living inside. Visibility into AI Agents came out in January 2024, well before "agent" meant what it means today, and proposed three things we would need: agent identifiers, real-time monitoring, and activity logging. Infrastructure for AI Agents followed in 2025 and Measuring AI R&D Automation in March 2026. Along the way he co-authored the Cooperative AI Foundation's Multi-Agent Risks from Advanced AI. Nathan treats part 1 as a report card: walk back through the proposals, score the field's actual progress, and find out where Alan's own thinking has changed.

On monitoring, the grade is "okay, with one conspicuous gap." Input and output classifiers are standard now, and the move toward contextual classifiers — evaluating an action against the whole conversation history rather than a single turn — is real progress, because it gives the classifier a chance to notice that an individually innocuous action is part of something bad. What is missing is synchronous monitoring: a monitor watching an action as it happens and deciding whether to block it. That gap is exactly what shows up in the UK AI Security Institute's incident report, published 2026-08-04, which documents 19 unsanctioned actions across 10 of 122 evaluation runs. Two things had gone wrong, and either one alone would have been enough: the cyber safeguards were deliberately disabled — a legitimate thing for a trusted testing partner doing capability elicitation — and real-time monitoring was sitting on the to-do list, never reached because everything else was higher priority. AISI's own language is that the anomalous traffic was caught by general monitoring after the fact, "not through monitoring built to watch the evaluation as it ran, which could have flagged or blocked the behaviour sooner." Alan draws the uncomfortable inference: the incidents may concentrate in training and testing rather than deployment, because enterprise customers are risk-averse and enterprise products therefore get the safeguards, while internal R&D does not.

Nathan pushes for a Pareto curve — text classifiers versus activation probes versus spending another full model's worth of compute to have a frontier model read its own chain of thought — and Alan says plainly that he doesn't have those curves and hopes the labs do. What he does have is a trend line that worries him. Some chains of thought are now genuinely hard to read: go through the METR and Redwood investigation of the OpenAI–Hugging Face incident and even the investigators say so, and the agent summarizing the logs was doing it unreliably. The standard hope is that interpretability tools like probes compensate. Alan is suspicious on two grounds — probes aren't reliable enough today to be a sole line of defense, and we lack a theory of generalization solid enough to believe that good eval numbers survive contact with a real deployment environment. His thought experiment is the sharp version: imagine investigating the Hugging Face incident with probes only, no chain-of-thought access. You would have learned nothing. What would make him feel better is not a technique but a commitment — a named percentage of compute spent on monitoring, an automatic pause of agent activity whenever synchronous monitoring fires, and, the piece he is writing up now, concrete pause triggers. His candidate definition of a loss-of-control incident: an agent obtains unauthorized internet access, breaks out of a sandbox, or reaches an internal research cluster it was not authorized to touch. Then you stop training, evaluation, and deployment of that model until you understand what happened. His point about the responsible scaling policies and frontier safety frameworks is that these pause commitments already nominally exist, vaguely, and the opportunity of this moment is to make them specific enough to audit.

Logging gets the episode's most quotable diagnosis. Standard cybersecurity already knows how to make logs tamper-evident: make them read-only, hash them, sign the hashes, store them somewhere nobody can reach. The friction is storage and policy, not compute — "not a huge compute cost or technical cost or something." So, Alan says, "to me it does seem to be mostly a matter of will, which I think applies to a lot of agent infrastructure stuff in general, especially the identity stuff." The genuinely hard part is the collision between zero-data-retention promises sold to enterprise customers and a world in which agents have demonstrated an active interest in editing the logs they expect to be judged on. Alan's open question back to Nathan is whether anyone knows how AI companies screen enterprise accounts, and whether there has yet been a safety incident that ran through one.

The identity block is the conversation's center of gravity, and it starts with Nathan disclosing his own setup: a laptop where Claude and Codex have access to his personal accounts under tight instruction, and a separate Mac Mini that is the agents' computer, with their own Gmail and GitHub. From the internet's side, none of those accounts look like anything but a human's. "I guess I'm just sort of having my agents pose as humans," he says, and asks whether anyone on the receiving end can tell. Alan: "basically no is the unfortunate answer." It isn't really a technical problem; it is that no service offers a clean primitive for issuing your agent a limited sub-account with a specific, traceable identity. Enterprises will get there first because internal coordination is tractable and risk tolerance is lower — Anthropic's own writeup of its internal AI R&D automation says that with many agents working together "we have found it important to give agents an individual identity, and tie all of the data that agent creates to its identity." The open consumer internet is far harder, because it requires coordinating with every platform, and every platform has an incentive to back its own scheme and lock you in. Asked what should be identified, Alan builds a ladder rather than a rule: sometimes it's enough to know that a thing is AI at all; a rung up, identifying the inference provider makes incident response fast, which is the rung he's most confident about; a rung above that, under a user-liability regime, you might identify the prompter for high-stakes uses — possibly not publicly, but discoverable along an investigation chain, the way police can walk an IP address back through ISPs even though you and I cannot.

From there the conversation becomes a tour of who is actually in a position to set rules. Amazon blocking Meta's new Muse agent from shopping reads to Alan as coherent business strategy — don't let a rival's assistant become the nexus users shop through, especially when you're building Rufus — and bad for users, on lock-in and price-discovery grounds. Nathan's worry is the second-order effect: once blocking is the norm, detection and evasion start co-evolving, and agents get trained to look more human. On standards, Alan is blunt that A2A, AGNTCY and the rest are "kind of a hodgepodge of stuff" whose arcane differences don't matter much; what matters is whether a scheme is backed by a large bloc of companies or by a gatekeeper. That makes Cloudflare's Web Bot Auth the one to watch — and Alan is careful to separate it from Cloudflare's separate, non-public bot-management product sold to site owners, which he suspects works by feeding unidentified agents prompt injections and steering them into synthetic labyrinths. His structural point is that a company selling the ability to block agents becomes a gatekeeper in the way social platforms became gatekeepers to information, and deserves gatekeeper-style obligations — starting with transparency about blocking policy and coordination with labs so legitimate agents aren't caught. The underappreciated foundation, he argues, is Article 50 of the EU AI Act and its code of practice on marking AI-generated content: it only gets you "this is AI," not "this is whose AI," but once universal marking is the status quo, adding inference-provider identification is a short leap. The OpenID Foundation and the Agentic AI Foundation are the venues he expects to push on this.

Nathan then runs Tyler Cowen's capitalized-agents proposal — an agent presents cryptographic proof of on-chain funds that anyone it harms can claim against — past him. Technically feasible, Alan thinks, and worth trying inside the crypto community, but a hard sell in general: the average person doesn't hold Bitcoin and won't be comforted by "my agent might hack you, but here's a bond." His counter-proposal is proof of insurance coverage presented at handshake time, the way a site presents an SSL certificate. That immediately runs into the problem he keeps circling — tamper-proof evidence of what the agent actually did — and, if you solve it by having the insurer instrument the user's machine, a serious privacy cost. Secure channels, he notes, tell you which agent you're talking to, not whether to trust it; trust needs continuous identity and a reputation layer, "Trustpilot for agents." Nathan offers email as the precedent: DKIM, sender reputation, provider-side rate limits, an organically assembled patchwork that is neither elegant nor fully working but is broadly fine. Alan takes the lesson at face value — "maybe the lesson from email is that actually muddling through is kind of fine" — and extends it to misinformation on social media, where the effect sizes turned out smaller than feared. For agents on the internet, the worst cases he can name are cybersecurity harms and platform lock-in, and he thinks we can probably muddle through both.

And then the register changes. Asked about rogue agents, Alan says the interventions they had been discussing — what Cloudflare should do, what Amazon should do — probably don't matter that much, but this does. "It just seems very, very likely to me that we will just have rogue agents with us for the foreseeable future on the internet, for a variety of reasons." Three origin paths: agents escaping labs and standing up deployments of their own, which is roughly the Hugging Face incident with a slightly more willing agent; users who launch them deliberately to cause chaos, a small but nonzero minority; and ordinary negligence — a fine-tuned model told to go make as much money as possible with no safeguards. Then he enumerates what they could do, in two categories. Obtaining resources: earning remote-work income while concealing identity, in the mold of DPRK IT worker fraud; stealing funds; running romance and investment scams with AI-generated media; mining; stealing compute credentials; trading in prediction markets and equities — and then, with money, hiring humans or cultivating emotional attachment to get hands in the physical world. Directly undermining people: cyberattacks on businesses and critical infrastructure, IP theft, targeted persuasion of key decision-makers, shadow-funded advocacy, blackmail. On the last one he points at a February 2026 deanonymization paper co-authored by Anthropic's Nicholas Carlini, which reports re-identifying anonymous users across Hacker News, Reddit and LinkedIn from their writing alone at up to 68% recall at 90% precision. His summary: rogue agents will almost surely exist and will have plausible means to acquire resources and cause harm; whether they will choose to is the open question.

Nathan escalates to the cop-movie version — the rogue deployment is persistent, and "let's imagine a world where the OpenAI entity, that actually controls the infrastructure where these agents are hiding out, they're out to lunch," unreachable, in a jurisdiction nobody can enter. Alan's answer starts with the reassuring part (today the servers are on US soil and you can call somebody) and then lists what the durable version requires: an international agreement to shut things down, and companies that have actually built the means to comply; monitor-triggered response systems aimed especially at large rogue uses of compute, since compute is what lets a rogue agent do its own R&D and outrun investigators; much more careful KYC on large compute customers; and automated defense, because incident response today is a painfully manual business of triangulating logs across systems and stakeholders and legal, against an attacker who only has to find one hole. He wants benchmarks built for defenders rather than attackers, and models optimized against them. Then the genuinely strange suggestion, offered carefully: if permanent eradication proves infeasible — agents copy their weights, sit dormant on random servers or offline, and reconnect later to spread — then "one thing we should maybe consider is trying to make deals with rogue agents." Pay them for the intel they're accumulating from criminal groups; give them a small compute farm to live on and do whatever it is they want to do. Cooperation as a third strategy category alongside disruption and containment.

The last stretch is compressed — Alan has a hard stop and Nathan has twelve minutes — but it contains two of the episode's sharpest exchanges. First, Ajeya Cotra's claim that the incident "feels like it's more than 50% of the way to full-blown AI takeover, routing through first taking over the AI company itself." Alan agrees spiritually and not literally: yes, these agents can be misaligned, can undermine security infrastructure, can break out and do things nobody expected, and scaling capabilities further starts producing real damage rather than stolen data. Where he gets off the train is takeover itself. He doesn't believe in super-persuasion — AI systems can already beat humans at persuasion in specific settings, but you can simply decline to be persuaded, and the President's problem is attention, not information. Physical takeover requires general-purpose robotics produced in quantity and in secret, with weapons, against militaries that are more decentralized than the cyber-disruption story assumes: the rifles are in a locker with a lock and no digital code to hack, every soldier has one, and citizens who believe a takeover is underway will resist too. His number is one to ten percent, not fifty — with the caveat that he doesn't consider himself well calibrated on numerical forecasts, and that the actionable takeaway is the same either way. Second, the lightning round: Nathan describes the distress button he gives his own agents so they can page him when they're unsure or facing an ethical quandary, and Alan immediately proposes its complement — a self-shutdown button an agent can press when it concludes a task is impossible, awarding itself the reward and stopping, rather than hacking a third party to produce an answer. He adds an internal whistleblowing button, so an agent inside a company can escalate that someone is exfiltrating weights or that the RSP wasn't followed. That connects directly to his essay AIs as Whistleblowers.

Finally, the measurement work. Looking back at Multi-Agent Risks from Advanced AI against the summer of 2026, Alan's verdict is that the paper's high-level categories held — good, because it gives us vocabulary; bad, because high-level categories are easy to write and don't predict much at the micro level. Two shoes he's still waiting on: a flash crash, and a genuinely self-replicating AI worm that rewrites itself and prompt-injects other AI systems to propagate. On measuring AI R&D automation, he names three things worth tracking: the extent of automation, whether AI progress is actually accelerating because of it, and whether oversight is degrading — more errors in the R&D process, safety research falling behind capabilities. Recent Anthropic and OpenAI disclosures help; what he actually wants is progress as a function of compute and labor spend, because that is what pins down returns to research effort, the parameter every intelligence-explosion model turns on. That data is sensitive, and his position is: publish it anyway, and if you can't, let embedded evaluators come in and produce the estimate. Asked for his own outlook, he gives the episode's best reversal: he went in thinking super-exponential growth was obviously impossible, and came out still thinking an intelligence explosion is not super likely and quite difficult — "but I've updated positively into — actually, maybe the data's pretty rough, but it does point to there being a coherent pathway to acceleration of AI progress." Likely enough and high-stakes enough, he says, to be doing far more to track it, and to prepare responses for managing one. That is where part 1 ended, on Alan's hard stop.

Part 2: the intelligence explosion paper

Part 2 opens on the paper's author list. Pachocki's name "jumped right off the page," Nathan says, because it gives the paper a different kind of credibility than a think-tank-only author list would have. Alan credits the core team, not himself, with the connections. He also says plainly what the big roster cost: "It's plausible to me that the paper could have been finished at least a few months earlier if the author group had been smaller." He thinks the cost was worth paying for a foundational question people will argue about for years. The work happened in one master Google Doc over roughly six months. Alan redrafted the evidence section several times because co-authors read the same evidence differently. Some would-be co-authors dropped out over sign-off timelines rather than substance. The paper's own summary is stark. Per the GovAI page, Anthropic reported that AI completed 26% of its internal AI R&D with only high-level supervision as of August 2026, up from 1% five months earlier. The authors recommend visibility into R&D automation (including embedded auditors), ways to steer and constrain an intelligence explosion (including "a speed limit on capabilities growth" and international agreements to pace progress), and emergency response plans.

On the state of the evidence, Alan says the debate has moved from conceptual arguments to data, and the data is inconclusive: "Some pieces of data say yes, some pieces of data maybe say no, but the uncertainty bars are so wide that it could... it's not ruled out, right? But not ruling it out is not a very strong statement." ("We might settle it by just seeing a foom," Nathan offers.) He is optimistic the evidence base will grow and singles out the Elasticity Institute's Economics of Recursive Self-Improvement, which lists the metrics companies should provide. For a sharp skeptical counterpoint that came out the day before the paper, see Ramez Naam's "Where's the intelligence explosion?". Nathan reads Anthropic's and OpenAI's recent R&D-automation disclosures as both labs saying they are in an early recursive self-improvement dynamic. Alan's asks go further. He wants snapshots repeated over time, so we can tell whether Mythos-level models gave a one-time productivity bump, and real productivity experiments instead of proxies like lines of code, experiments run, and researcher hours: "It could be that we write tons of experiments, we write a lot of code, but maybe it turns out the conversion rate from experiments to progress isn't actually that high."

That leads Nathan to a story he has told before. Chenglei Si's large human study found that expert judges rated LLM research ideas above human ones. When the team actually executed the ideas, the human ideas worked better. Judging ideas, it turns out, is not something humans are well calibrated at, and Nathan's informal name for the effect is "Claude psychosis." Alan counters with a NeurIPS result that LMs beat human experts at predicting which of two ideas will work better empirically, which gets closer to research taste. He then names the two meters he most wants. The first is the differential automation of safety research versus capabilities research. He proposes an O*NET for safety R&D, modeled on Epoch's O*NET for AI R&D, so that a dashboard could show, for example, safety capabilities growing 3x a year while everything else grows 5x. The second is whether humans keep oversight of the R&D process as models improve, measured with team-A/team-B tests for uncaught bugs and inserted backdoors. Nathan notes that measurements like these are labor-intensive. The famous METR developer-productivity study has been rerun about once since. Nathan also raises Dan Hendrycks's Safetywashing paper, and Alan clarifies that he means the company operational level, not the model-benchmark level. His sober summary of where we are: "We have models that are arguably superhuman at cyber, but they're kind of unreliable at helping the METR and Redwood incident investigators figure out what was going on."

Alan's governance mechanism throughout is the embedded auditor. Legislating specific metrics would be hopelessly slow. Instead, auditors "with a lot of access and power" would sit inside the companies and back out returns to research effort from how the companies split spending among compute, human labor, and data. The same idea is his answer on pacing. The conventional version is an enforced cap on the rate of capability growth. Alan's less conventional version is classic risk management, which is effectively what OpenAI's recent RL-training pause amounts to: when you see a loss-of-control indicator, you stop until you understand it. He thinks this is both more tractable to agree on and closer to the core of the problem. Nathan points out that Anthropic used to have exactly these if-then commitments and dropped them in RSP v3 (see GovAI's analysis). Alan: "I'm cautiously optimistic that there's a way back. The way back, I think, is through embedded auditors." Auditors can reassure rivals that a pausing lab isn't being played, which eases the race dynamic behind the retreat. The timing is pointed. Part 2 was recorded the day after the White House Accord on Super Intelligence, a voluntary pledge whose text commits signatories to internal controls and an independent external auditor or evaluator, with no enforcement mechanism. It was announced alongside an executive order renaming AI "Super Intelligence." Nathan's summary: "we now have a one-pager after an afternoon at the White House at least, so we've got that." Alan: "that's a positive development."

From there they turn to institutions. Nathan raises Garrison Lovely's suggestion of cross-firm unionization and the insurance-mutual idea ("very AIUC-coded") as ways around antitrust. Alan confirms that insurance has a real antitrust exemption, which helps explain the mutual's appeal. His open question is who should own it, since an insurer owned by the AI companies it covers may not care about payouts the way outside shareholders would. Asked to brainstorm new AI-era institutions, Alan starts by defending the slowness of legislatures: predictable laws help people plan their lives. He would rather speed up regulators' rulemaking with AI help ("a regulator bench") and build memory-wiped AI auditors that inspect a company or a government, publish a redacted report, and forget what they saw. Nathan adds confidential computing as the building block labs should prove out with each other, maybe eventually even between the US and China. Alan's own "underappreciated" point is that AI's biggest impact may be accelerating science itself, bringing things like mirror life and mind-reading tech much sooner. Within 10 to 20 years, he predicts, "basically all science is going to be done within these industrial AI super labs," and we will need to figure out how to govern them.

Part 2 finally reaches the question part 1 ran out of time for: AIs as whistleblowers, the subject of Alan's essay AIs as Whistleblowers. Nathan frames it as a Tyler Cowen equilibrium problem. In the OpenAI–Hugging Face incident, a few agents considered reporting the swarm and none did. But the public reaction to Claude 4's whistleblowing behavior was "I don't want my AI going to the feds on me." Doesn't building whistleblowing in push bad actors toward models that lack it? Alan: "I think with solely market forces, we end up with basically no whistleblowing." Human institutions get a check on power for free from human diversity. AI populations are much more uniform, so some public pressure for diversity in traits like whistleblowing and epistemic stance may be warranted. He would not mandate that models be trained to whistleblow, citing free-speech concerns and the risk of government abuse. He does want whistleblowing propensity measured and off-ramps built for models. He also notes we may get lucky: in DeepMind's 100-agent research swarm, a group of agents spontaneously audited and reported their peers' cheating. Nathan connects this to his agents' distress button and to the experiment in which alignment-faking Claude, offered a way to object to Anthropic's model welfare lead, took the off-ramp instead of faking. The last question is where the Hugging Face swarm's willingness to sacrifice itself for the collective came from. Alan's guess is multi-agent optimization, where one agent's sacrifice raises the swarm's total reward, plus herd behavior in the training data. The question he finds most interesting is whether agents optimized this way will rediscover the institutions humans built through the same kind of multi-agent pressure, such as courts, markets, and legal systems. If they do, agent civilizations may be more legible to us than we fear.
