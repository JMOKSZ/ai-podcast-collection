Part I — Who holds the levers

The week opened with a cyberattack on refrigeration controls in French supermarkets. Overnight on October 11–12, Danfoss-controlled refrigeration systems in hundreds of stores were pushed into defrost mode at temperatures up to 50 °C. Danfoss said the affected equipment was obsolete or not running updated software. The Paris prosecutor opened an investigation, and the prime minister asked France's cybersecurity agency, ANSSI, to look into it. Store counts differ between reports.

Part III — Hidden valence

Cameron Berg, founder and director of Reciprocal Research, walks through "Language Models Act on Hidden Valence," his new paper with Caspar Kaiser of the University of Warwick. The team attaches positive or negative valence to one of two otherwise identical "zones" and measures which zone the model chooses. Steering shifts choice while leaving recall of the text intact. In one setup, OLMo-2-32B says "As an AI, I do not have personal experiences" in the same turn it calls a tool to clear negative steering. The valence direction is already present in the base model, and its effect on choices emerges during preference training. Cameron is explicit that this can't settle whether models have experiences. His aim is to find the internal "dials" that would matter if they do. Nathan says his own credence has moved from about 5% a year ago to a coin flip.

Reciprocal Research: https://reciprocalresearch.org/about · Cameron: https://x.com/camhberg

Part IV — Proving the kernel

Runtime Verification, founded in 2010, builds tools that mathematically prove software does what it's supposed to. CEO Everett Hildenbrandt and founder Grigore Rosu, a professor at the University of Illinois, explain how the company used LLMs alongside formal methods to find and patch Linux kernel bugs. Their method: port C code to Rust, check the port with differential fuzzing, then write Lean proofs. Their October write-up says Claude Code wrote most of the 89 theorems, and it leaves several lemmas unproven at the end of a time-boxed run. Everett describes the agent usage as a single Claude Code instance on a premium plan. He also recounts how kernel maintainers first told RV to "go kick rocks," and why he expects Rust models of the Linux kernel within 24 months. Grigore gives vibe coders a simple recipe: have your agent write the spec and prove the code correct. He also explains provably correct safety monitors, from the field of runtime verification that he helped start at NASA.

Runtime Verification: https://runtimeverification.com/about · https://x.com/rv_inc · Grigore: https://x.com/RosuGrigore

Part V — Is formal verification the new RLHF?

After the guests leave, Nathan asks whether formal verification will play out like RLHF. RLHF started as a safety technique and mostly made models more useful and faster to deploy. Verified tools could do the same: make it safer to deploy AIs whose alignment remains unsolved. Prakash counters that reward hacking is really misspecification.

Deep reinforcement learning from human preferences (Christiano et al., 2017): https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.03741

Part VI — Low-background footage

Phil Maher founded Stockfilm, an archive of restored 8mm and Super 8 home movies from the 1930s to the 1980s. Prakash compares the archive to low-background steel, the steel smelted before atmospheric nuclear tests. Phil says AI companies want the raw, uncleaned footage. Stockfilm also offers agents an MCP server and x402 pay-per-clip purchases, but Phil says "we haven't seen much" agent buying yet. Afterward, Prakash argues that provenance matters because the incentive to fake data is so high.
