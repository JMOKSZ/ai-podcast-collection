Joel Predd, Ben Boudreaux, and Matt Chessen are from RAND's Center for the Geopolitics of AGI. They join Nathan to walk through their September 2026 Perspective, A U.S. Strategy to Secure Geopolitical Advantage on an Uncertain Path to Superintelligence: Maintaining Freedom of Action. The team also summarized it on Substack. Nathan first asks whether governments are taking AI seriously. Joel recalls that when the center launched, "we kind of had to whisper 'AGI'". Now "you can't get into a meeting without calling it superintelligence." Ben says the U.S. government is more aware of the technology's strategic stakes, but neither it nor the frontier companies has settled on a strategy. Not building long-lead capabilities like verification, evaluation, technical expertise, and crisis communication "itself might be a type of choice that forecloses options we might need down the line."

Matt gives the bottom line first: there is too much uncertainty to justify any single superintelligence strategy. The work builds on the team's earlier Finding Common Ground in AGI Strategy Debates. Its diagnosis is that the stakes include decisive geopolitical advantage and "humanity's survival with agency." Its prescription is to build a human–AI ecosystem plus a security architecture stable enough to build it in. Surveying the proposals in circulation, the team found seven archetypes, each with a coherent win state and a pathology. Dominance risks extreme power concentration and value lock-in. Co-development through a U.S.-led consortium that probably includes China risks making the founders' values the default. Preparedness can normalize accumulating damage. Moratorium, the position of Eliezer Yudkowsky and MIRI (see If Anyone Builds It, Everyone Dies), risks enforcement hardening into global surveillance. Deterrence, as in Hendrycks, Schmidt, and Wang's MAIM proposal, risks an eternal standoff. Continuity of society risks humanity surviving "as a relic." Acceleration risks markets that don't price in harms. A classifier of five "pivot factor" questions sorts people into these camps: Is danger close? Is coexistence feasible? Is restraint feasible? Is a decisive strategic advantage reachable? Is suppression feasible? Matt notes there is no consensus on the answers, the evidence won't settle them on the timeline required, and meanwhile "we may be running an acceleration strategy by default." The team chose "coexistence" over "alignment" on purpose. Alignment is one path among several, alongside control, architectural schemes, and "methods not yet known."

Nathan pushes hardest on dominance. He finds the endgame of Machines of Loving Grace implausible, and he recounts playing China in a fast-takeoff tabletop exercise. Joel separates technological, economic, and military versions of decisive advantage. He cites colleague Tobias Sytsma's Decisive Economic Advantage and Philip Root's game-theory paper. In Root's model, a race defaults to mutual sabotage over cooperation unless AI yields a "wonder weapon," a capability that discontinuously shifts the military balance, as Joel's earlier work framed it. Joel notes some have argued that Mythos-class cyber models were the first of the AI era. Ben adds that a fast takeoff extends a leader's lead but also strains the leader's control, and that any dominance may erode through theft, distillation, or diffusion. The most striking finding is about people, not technology. Across hundreds of RAND games, including U.S.–China strategy games like Breakwater, players' going-in priors decide outcomes: "your perceptions about the facts of the matter matter as much, if not more, than the actual facts of the matter." Los Alamos could run a calculation before Trinity, Joel says, but nobody can run one today on whether danger is close. He also reads nuclear history as freedom of action in slow motion: a sprint, a monopoly, moratorium proposals, verifiable agreements, then counterproliferation. Policymakers then had time to build options as they went, and this time we won't.

Nathan states his own view that a wise species would choose co-development with a moratorium as the fallback. Matt's answer: humanity isn't a unitary actor, and co-development with China risks "locking in non-democratic founding values." Joel stresses that this is a strategy for America, not only Washington. Secured data centers and incident reporting can be markets and civil-society functions. Ben ties this to resilience, preserving human capacities so that even a moratorium stays feasible. Then the team walks through the objectives. Ben covers the human–AI ecosystem: alignment and control research, evals that catch sandbagging, containment for untrusted models, and human checkpoints on high-stakes decisions. With Alvin Moon he is developing longitudinal "agency evals" (see A Formal Model of How AI Erodes Human Agency). Joel describes adapting legacy institutions: counterterrorism, intelligence, the conventional military (Zachary Burdette's project), the nuclear enterprise (Edward Geist), and biosecurity (RAND's defense-in-depth report). Matt explains that much of an AI security architecture will be built by the private sector. The point is to build long-lead capabilities like verification even though not every strategy needs them, and above all "navigation capabilities" that show where we are in the strategy space.

On cost, Joel quotes a peer reviewer: "a strategy without a price tag is just a vision." His reply is that every archetype except acceleration means a society-level reorientation on the scale of the early nuclear era or post-9/11. The gap between any of them and the status quo dwarfs the gap between any one of them and freedom of action. Matt adds that much of the cost falls outside the federal budget. The team is running a sprint to estimate lead times and costs for a 106-item Security Architecture Catalog, drawing partly on RAND CAST. Ben offers four criteria for any policy: which strategies it forecloses and how reversibly, which it opens, what information it yields, and what it signals. Export controls are his example of a foreclosure that may still be reversible. Nathan quotes the report's call to "signal to hold deterrence and cooperation credibly open at once." Matt explains that shared evaluation and verification serve both sides, while offensive counter-AI tools call for discretion. He warns that chip controls may have made a domestic chip industry "existential" for China, and that signaling a U.S. monopoly could be used against the CCP is among the most dangerous moves available. Ben points to the Cold War as evidence that an open society's messy debate is a strength.

Joel left at his planned hard stop, and Ben and Matt carried the final stretch. Ben's neglected priority is holding AIs at risk: credible shutdown capacity, which depends on being able to live without these systems. Nathan tells a story about Claude rotating the password on his own agent console, and Ben shares his personal practice: "the human comes in at the beginning, and then it comes in at the end." He also favors manual fail-safes, paper records, AI-free spaces like LAUSD's student AI moratorium, and an "AI Sabbath." Matt's neglected priority is the typology of superintelligence. Most strategies assume a singleton, but superintelligent behavior could emerge from swarms of sub-AGI agents. He points to this summer's agent swarm hacks (see Swarm Traces and the Hugging Face incident timeline) and RAND's The Ecology of AI Risk. Nathan suggests a red line against training "AI generals." Ben would instead lean on the law of armed conflict and human accountability. Matt's red line is rushing AI into nuclear command and control. In closing, Ben lists what he's watching: pacing proposals, evaluator access, lab security for internal deployments, recursive self-improvement, and a U.S.–China incident channel. Matt expects "a rough patch in the cyber world over the next twelve to twenty-four months" as Mythos-class capabilities reach open-weight models. He also urges newcomers to start with the report's pivot-factor table, which he presented at The Curve.
